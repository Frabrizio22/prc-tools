Price Per Mg: The Real Cost of 'Cheap' Peptides

Published March 19, 2026 | 6 min read

You find two vendors selling "the same" peptide. Vendor A charges $30. Vendor B charges $50. The choice seems obvious—until you receive both vials and discover the $30 "bargain" contains half the active compound.

Welcome to the peptide pricing shell game. In an unregulated market where labeling standards don't exist and verification is optional, price-per-vial means nothing. The only number that matters is price per milligram of actual peptide.

This article will teach you the one calculation that separates informed buyers from victims of creative pricing—and why transparent vendors display this number prominently.

The Concentration Trap

Here's how the game works. Two vendors sell "Semaglutide 5mg":

Vendor A: $30 Label: "Semaglutide 5mg" Actual: 5mg peptide in 5mg lyophilized powder Concentration: 100% (5mg ÷ 5mg = 1.0) Price per mg: $30 ÷ 5mg = $6.00/mg Vendor B: $25 Label: "Semaglutide 5mg Vial" Actual: 2.5mg peptide + 2.5mg mannitol filler Concentration: 50% (2.5mg ÷ 5mg = 0.5) Price per mg: $25 ÷ 2.5mg = $10.00/mg

Vendor B's "cheaper" vial costs 67% more per milligram of semaglutide. Both technically contain "5mg vials"—one measures total powder weight, the other measures active peptide content. Without COAs specifying net peptide content, you can't know which you're buying.

This isn't a hypothetical example. A 2024 analysis of 47 research peptide vendors found that only 18% clearly disclosed whether advertised weights referred to net peptide or total lyophilized mass. The ambiguity is profitable—and intentional.

The One Calculation You Need

Evaluating true peptide cost requires one formula:

Price Per Milligram = Total Price ÷ Net Peptide Content (mg)

Net peptide content comes from HPLC purity reports, not product labels. If a vendor sells "BPC-157 10mg" for $40 with 97% purity:

Net peptide = 10mg × 0.97 = 9.7mg Price per mg = $40 ÷ 9.7mg = $4.12/mg

If that same vendor offered no COA, you'd assume 10mg and calculate $4.00/mg—a 3% error. Manageable. But if they filled half the vial with mannitol and you assumed 10mg net peptide when reality was 5mg, you'd calculate $4.00/mg while actually paying $8.00/mg—a 100% pricing error.

This is why COA-verified net peptide content is non-negotiable for price comparison. Anything else is guesswork.

Why Vendors Hide Price Per Mg

Transparency benefits buyers but threatens vendors competing on perceived rather than actual value. Consider the incentives:

Scenario 1: Opaque Pricing

  • Vendor sells "BPC-157 10mg" for $35
  • No COA provided; buyer assumes 10mg net peptide
  • Actual content: 5mg peptide + 5mg mannitol (50% pure)
  • Perceived price: $3.50/mg
  • Actual price: $7.00/mg
  • Vendor profit: Doubled through information asymmetry

Scenario 2: Transparent Pricing

  • Vendor sells "BPC-157 5mg (verified)" for $35
  • COA shows 97% purity = 4.85mg net peptide
  • Listed price per mg: $7.22/mg
  • Buyer compares to competitor offering $6.50/mg
  • Vendor forced to compete on actual value

Opaque pricing protects margins. Transparent pricing drives competition on quality and efficiency. The market equilibrium favors vendors who benefit from confusion.

That's why calculating price per milligram yourself—from verified COA data—remains the only reliable method.

How to Verify Any Vendor's True Pricing

Use this three-step process for any peptide purchase:

Step 1: Demand the COA

Request third-party HPLC analysis (Janoshik, Lab4Tox, Condensed Matter, etc.). If the vendor won't provide it, walk away. No COA = no way to verify net peptide content = no way to calculate true price.

Step 2: Extract Net Peptide Content

Look for the purity percentage in the HPLC report. Multiply label weight by purity:

Net Peptide (mg) = Label Weight × (Purity % ÷ 100) Example: 10mg vial × 96.5% purity = 9.65mg net peptide

Step 3: Calculate Price Per Mg

Divide total cost by net peptide milligrams:

Price Per Mg = Vial Price ÷ Net Peptide (mg) Example: $45 ÷ 9.65mg = $4.66/mg

Compare this number—not vial prices—across vendors. The lowest price per milligram of verified peptide wins.
